# Capable 55 — Evidence Base

- **Status:** Living evidence document
- **Created:** 2026-08-30
- **Purpose:** Maintain the research rationale behind the Capable 55 training system so that the program can be updated when better evidence appears.

> This document is not the program itself. It records *why* the program is designed the way it is, how strong the evidence is, and what would make us change our mind.

---

## 1. Core design principle

Capable 55 is designed for a previously highly trained athlete returning after a substantial period of detraining. It is therefore deliberately *not* based only on population averages for adults over 50.

The working model is:

- chronological age matters, but is only one input;
- training history, prior performance, current detraining, injury history, recovery capacity and present tolerance matter more for day-to-day prescription;
- the goal is broad physical capability rather than optimization of a single sport;
- strength, aerobic fitness, power, skill/gymnastics, mobility, balance/coordination, recovery and real-world endurance are trained concurrently;
- training should be progressively rebuilt rather than attempting to reproduce historical training loads immediately;
- methods may change; principles and measurement history should persist.

### Evidence anchor — master athletes

McKendry et al. systematically reviewed 55 studies of chronically trained master athletes. Long-term training was associated with substantially better preservation of physical function, maximal aerobic capacity, strength and body composition than seen in typical older comparison groups.

**Why it matters:** ordinary age norms are a poor ceiling for someone with a long athletic history. They remain useful as health/reference floors, but not as the main target.

**Evidence strength:** systematic review/meta-analysis; good anchor, although most included athletes were older than the current Capable 55 subject.

- [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29715523/)
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arr.2018.04.007)

## 2. Retraining after detraining

A central assumption of Block 1 is that previously developed capacity can return substantially with structured retraining, but not every tissue or performance quality returns at the same rate.

### 2024 master-athlete detraining/retraining case study

Lepers et al. followed a highly trained 53.8-year-old master triathlete through 12 weeks of detraining followed by 12 weeks of retraining.

After detraining:

- cycling VO2max fell about 9.1%;
- running VO2max fell about 10.9%;
- peak cycling power fell 12.7%;
- maximal aerobic running velocity fell 8.6%;
- knee-extensor strength fell about 8.2%;
- body fat rose from 10.5% to 13.8%;
- lean mass fell by 2.2 kg.

After 12 weeks of progressive retraining, almost all measured variables returned to baseline or slightly above it, except running economy and lean mass.

**Why it matters:** it supports the plausibility of a rapid return of cardiorespiratory fitness in an experienced master athlete while also warning us that some qualities can lag behind. It is one person, so it is illustrative rather than a basis for precise forecasts.

**Evidence strength:** case study — highly relevant phenotype, weak causal/generalizable evidence.

- [Full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1508642/full)
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2024.1508642)

### Practical implication

The first 12-week block is a **retraining block**, not a beginner program. However, the return of skill, cardiovascular capacity and muscular force should not be interpreted as proof that connective-tissue tolerance has recovered equally quickly.

This is why early training uses submaximal strength work, controlled gymnastics exposure and progressive impact/kipping rather than immediate pursuit of old numbers.

## 3. Why strength and aerobic training are done together

Capable 55 aims to preserve/build both strength and cardiorespiratory fitness instead of alternating long single-quality phases.

Khalafi et al. reviewed 49 studies involving 2,587 middle-aged and older adults. Concurrent aerobic + resistance training improved cardiorespiratory fitness compared with resistance training alone and improved strength compared with aerobic training alone. Importantly, the magnitude of VO2max improvement was not significantly worse than aerobic training alone, and strength gains were not significantly worse than resistance training alone.

**Why it matters:** with a roughly five-hour weekly budget, simultaneous development is reasonable rather than assuming a large “interference effect” makes concurrent training unsuitable.

**Evidence strength:** systematic review/meta-analysis of randomized trials. Population is less trained than the Capable 55 subject, so it supports the principle rather than exact programming.

- [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35728627/)
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2022.113888)

### Practical implication

The default week combines:

- two strength-oriented exposures;
- aerobic low-intensity work;
- one higher-intensity/mixed CrossFit exposure;
- an engine/adventure exposure;
- small doses of skill/power work.

The exact distribution should change if data show that recovery or progress is inadequate.

## 4. Why power is a separate target

Strength is not enough for the Capable 55 objective. The desired outcome includes descending stairs quickly, reacting, jumping, learning new sports and moving with “youthful” speed.

A systematic review/meta-analysis by Straight et al./related 2022 evidence comparing power training with traditional strength training in older adults found larger gains in muscle power and small-to-moderate advantages in activity-based performance measures.

Another 2022 systematic review/meta-analysis in healthy community-dwelling older adults found a modest improvement in physical function from power training compared with traditional strength training, although certainty was low.

**Why it matters:** muscle power — producing force quickly — is meaningfully different from maximal force. It justifies keeping jumps, throws, fast concentric actions and other safe explosive work in the system.

**Evidence strength:** systematic reviews/meta-analyses, but mostly adults >60 and not trained masters; therefore we use the principle, not geriatric exercise prescriptions.

- [Effectiveness of power training vs strength training](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35953775/)
- [Power vs traditional strength training and physical function](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35544136/)
- [High-velocity power training review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37328359/)

### Practical implication

Power exposure starts low-risk and low-fatigue:

- standing jump or vertical jump;
- medicine-ball throw where available;
- controlled explosive pulling;
- fast concentric intent with suitable loads;
- later, more sport-specific reactive work.

Power is not trained to exhaustion.

## 5. CrossFit, gymnastics and shoulder/knee risk

The program preserves CrossFit because it supplies intensity, broad movement exposure, skill and enjoyment. But gymnastics and high-intensity movements are scaled during rebuilding.

### Injury distribution

A systematic review of 25 CrossFit injury studies (12,079 participants) found the shoulder, spine and knee to be the most frequently injured regions. Reported incidence varied widely across studies.

- [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33322981/)

A 2023 orthopaedic review similarly identified shoulder, spine and knee as common injury sites and reported that improper form and aggravation of previous injury were frequent causes. It also reported fewer injuries where athletes trained with supervision/coaching.

- [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37155727/)
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5435/JAAOS-D-22-01219)

**Why it matters:** the target of six consecutive CrossFit-style/kipping muscle-ups is retained, but shoulder capacity and technique are prerequisites rather than afterthoughts.

**Evidence strength:** injury epidemiology is observational and heterogeneous. It identifies recurring patterns, not a precise individual injury probability.

### Practical implication

Before accumulating kipping/muscle-up volume:

1. establish pain-free usable shoulder range;
2. establish scapular control;
3. rebuild strict pulling strength;
4. rebuild hollow/arch control;
5. add controlled kip volume;
6. add transition practice;
7. only then pursue repeated full muscle-ups.

The PT is used partly as a movement-quality and load-tolerance sensor, not merely as a source of hard workouts.

## 6. Load management: avoid both spikes and long inactivity

A 2024 narrative review on extended-career athletes emphasized balancing age-related changes with accumulated expertise, previous injury and individual load tolerance. It recommended load-response monitoring, strength training, broad movement exposure, recovery management, and avoiding both large workload peaks and prolonged inactivity.

**Why it matters:** this is a better conceptual fit than treating an experienced athlete as simply “an older adult.” It supports gradual progression after detraining and regular adaptation to the user's response.

**Evidence strength:** narrative review (lower certainty than meta-analysis) and focused on extended-career/elite athletes rather than masters, but conceptually relevant.

- [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11556553/)
- [PMID](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39385401/)
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/19417381241285870)

### Practical implication

Capable 55 uses 8–12 week review cycles. Training load is increased based on tolerance and trajectory, not on historical identity.

A bad month, shoulder flare-up or disruption should lead to a lower-dose version of the system rather than an all-or-nothing break whenever possible.

## 7. Sleep as a performance variable

Current sleep is around six hours, while the subject believes performance and wellbeing are better closer to eight hours. This is treated as a major modifiable constraint.

### Athlete sleep consensus

Walsh et al.'s expert consensus notes that short sleep (<7 h/night) is common in athletes and argues against a rigid one-size-fits-all sleep prescription. Individual perceived need, training, travel, stress and other factors should be considered.

**Why it matters:** the program should not mechanically impose “8 hours because guidelines say so.” The correct target is established through repeated observation of sleep duration, daytime function, training response and perceived need.

- [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33144349/)
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102025)

### Sleep extension and naps

A systematic review of 25 athlete sleep-intervention studies found sleep extension and naps to be the most consistently promising interventions for physical and cognitive performance. In the included sleep-extension studies, increasing habitual sleep by roughly 46–113 minutes was associated with improved performance outcomes.

**Why it matters:** moving gradually from ~6 hours toward 7+ and then evaluating whether further extension helps is evidence-aligned.

- [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10354314/)
- [PMID](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37462808/)

### Practical implication

Track:

- bedtime;
- wake time;
- estimated sleep duration;
- morning energy (1–5);
- optionally resting heart rate.

Treat sleep improvement as an experiment rather than as a moral score.

## 8. Caffeine cutoff as an N=1 experiment

The current noon caffeine cutoff is based primarily on the user's own repeated observation that caffeine after lunch worsens sleep. It is *not* presented as a universal evidence-derived threshold.

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis examined caffeine consumed before late-afternoon/evening athletic activity. Across ten small studies (128 athletes), evening caffeine produced a small reduction in sleep efficiency, while total sleep time showed a non-significant trend toward reduction. Subjective sleep impairment was consistently reported. The authors stressed the small evidence base and predominance of male participants.

**Why it matters:** caffeine can plausibly impair sleep, but the exact cutoff should be individualized. Noon is a testable personal rule, not a biological constant.

**Evidence strength:** systematic review/meta-analysis but small evidence base.

- [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41003623/)
- [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12473705/)
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports13090317)

### Current experiment

**Hypothesis:** eliminating caffeine after 12:00 improves sleep duration/quality enough to improve morning energy and training consistency.

**Initial test period:** two weeks.

**Decision rule:** retain, relax or tighten the cutoff based on repeated personal data rather than ideology.

## 9. Why body composition is a guardrail, not the main objective

Baseline Jackson–Pollock 3-site estimate: 17.7% body fat.

The estimate is useful primarily for *trend tracking*. Skinfold equations carry measurement and model error, so apparent small changes should not automatically trigger dietary or training changes.

Capable 55 prioritizes:

1. functional capacity;
2. training consistency;
3. recovery;
4. strength/power/endurance;
5. movement robustness;
6. body composition as a secondary output/guardrail.

### Practical implication

Repeat the same three-site method under similar conditions every ~8 weeks. Prefer repeated trend to single-point precision.

No specific body-fat target is currently evidence-justified for this individual's performance goals.

## 10. Why mobility is treated as usable range, not stretching scores

The aim is not maximal passive flexibility. The aim is enough active, controllable range to:

- reach strong overhead positions;
- squat and hinge comfortably;
- tolerate gymnastics;
- run, ski, paddle and learn new sports;
- reduce movement workarounds caused by stiffness.

For the first block, mobility is therefore assessed through usable movement positions and joint-specific restrictions rather than a large battery of passive flexibility tests.

**Current evidence status:** this section needs a deeper targeted review. The general role of range of motion and movement-specific capacity is well supported, but we have not yet built a sufficiently strong evidence set to claim that any particular mobility routine prevents injury.

**Program rule:** do not make strong injury-prevention claims for stretching alone.

## 11. Balance and coordination

Static eyes-closed single-leg balance is already strong at baseline:

- left: at least 79 seconds (test stopped voluntarily);
- right: 50 seconds (balance lost).

This means static balance should not consume much training time simply because the user is over 50.

### Practical implication

Future balance work should shift toward:

- unilateral strength;
- dynamic balance;
- landing and deceleration;
- reactive movement;
- handstand skill;
- sport-specific coordination.

Population norms remain useful as reference points, but a test with a low ceiling is not useful for driving training in a high-functioning individual.

## 12. Why 2,000 m row is used as an engine benchmark

The user has a meaningful historical reference (7:30) and rowing permits hard aerobic testing without the impact load of an immediate maximal 5 km run after detraining.

The 2k row is therefore used as:

- a repeatable whole-body engine benchmark;
- a bridge to historical athletic identity;
- a lower-impact hard test while running volume is rebuilt.

It is **not** assumed to measure every aspect of aerobic fitness or adventure endurance.

### Practical implication

Record:

- time;
- average watts;
- average heart rate if available;
- maximum heart rate if available;
- pacing;
- RPE;
- next-day response.

The first return test should be hard but does not need to be a maximal “death test.”

---

## 13. Why running returns progressively

The desired endpoint is not primarily a fast 5 km; it is the ability to spontaneously run ~15 km without the body vetoing the decision.

Historical 5 km performance (20:00) shows strong prior running capacity, but it should not dictate immediate training load after the current low-volume period.

### Practical implication

Running exposure increases only as knee/foot/ankle tolerance permits. Early aerobic volume can be supplied by cycling, rowing, SkiErg, hiking and paddling while impact tolerance catches up.

A later 5 km benchmark can be retained, but the higher-level success measure is broad adventure/run readiness.

---

## 14. Why real-world adventure capacity is measured

Laboratory and gym measures are proxies. The actual north-star goal is choice: being able to say yes to hiking, paddling, skiing, surfing, long mountain days and other adventures.

Therefore the system includes an intentionally less standardized **real-world capacity test**, initially a ~90-minute low-to-moderate intensity outing.

Track:

- duration;
- modality;
- approximate intensity;
- physical state at 60 and 90 minutes;
- next-morning fatigue/stiffness;
- any limiting body part.

This measure is deliberately ecological rather than laboratory-pure.

## 15. Screening and safety

The program avoids unnecessary maximal testing in the first return phase.

For exercise preparticipation screening, the modern ACSM approach focuses substantially on:

- current physical activity;
- known cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease;
- signs or symptoms;
- intended exercise intensity.

A previously inactive but asymptomatic person does not automatically need medical clearance for light-to-moderate exercise, while exertional warning symptoms warrant medical assessment before hard testing.

**Practical red flags before maximal/hard testing include** new or unexplained chest discomfort, fainting/syncope, unusual exertional breathlessness, or concerning palpitations.

Reference:

- Riebe et al. *Updating ACSM's Recommendations for Exercise Preparticipation Health Screening.* Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise (2015).

This is a safety framework, not an individualized medical clearance.

## 16. Evidence → program map

| Program choice | Evidence/rationale |
|---|---|
| Treat as retraining, not generic 50+ fitness | Master-athlete literature + prior training history |
| 12-week rebuild block | Retraining evidence + practical review horizon |
| Strength + aerobic concurrently | Concurrent-training meta-analysis |
| Keep power as its own quality | Power-training meta-analyses |
| Retain CrossFit but scale early | CrossFit injury epidemiology + enjoyment/specificity |
| Delay high-volume kipping/muscle-ups | Shoulder injury pattern + current detraining |
| Use PT for assessment/technique | Supervision evidence + high information value |
| Avoid immediate historical maxes | Load-management principles after detraining |
| 2k row before maximal 5k run | Lower impact + meaningful historical benchmark |
| Track sleep and work upward from ~6 h | Athlete sleep consensus + intervention review |
| Noon caffeine cutoff | Strong personal signal + supporting caffeine/sleep evidence |
| Body fat = trend/guardrail | Measurement uncertainty + performance-first objective |
| Static balance de-emphasized | Baseline already high; low-ceiling metric |
| Real-world adventure session | Direct measure of north-star capability |
| Review every 8–12 weeks | Adaptive load management + individual response |

## 17. Current evidence confidence

### High confidence

- Regular endurance and resistance training are both valuable.
- Concurrent training is a viable way to develop both in this age range.
- Sleep matters materially to recovery/performance.
- Large jumps from detraining directly to old training loads are unnecessary risk.
- Power deserves explicit training rather than relying only on slow strength work.

### Moderate confidence

- The proposed weekly distribution is a good match for this individual's goals and five-hour budget.
- A 12-week initial block is long enough to generate useful signal.
- Progressive gymnastics exposure is preferable to immediately testing repeated muscle-ups.
- Sleep extension from the current ~6 h is likely to be beneficial.

### Individual hypotheses still being tested

- 12:00 is the optimal caffeine cutoff.
- Which balance/mobility limitations actually constrain performance.
- The best mix of CrossFit classes vs dedicated strength/engine sessions.
- Exact running progression that gives the fastest safe return.
- Ideal body weight/body-fat range for performance.
- How quickly historical rowing, running, deadlift and gymnastics capacity return.

## 18. Evidence gaps / next searches

This document should not pretend the research review is finished. Priority gaps:

1. **Mobility/flexibility:** best evidence on active range, resistance training through full ROM, stretching, injury risk and performance.
2. **Tendon/connective-tissue adaptation after detraining/retraining:** especially implications for masters athletes.
3. **Running return-to-load after prolonged undertraining:** evidence relevant to previously trained middle-aged runners.
4. **Gymnastics/kipping shoulder load:** direct biomechanical evidence where available.
5. **Dose-response for strength in trained masters athletes:** minimum effective volume under a five-hour total weekly budget.
6. **VO2max/interval training in masters athletes:** optimal high-intensity dose and recovery.
7. **Training and mental wellbeing:** useful exercise effects without turning the program into psychiatric treatment.
8. **Long-term adventure readiness:** proxy tests that predict hiking/mountain/paddling capacity.

## 19. Update protocol

When new evidence is added:

1. record the citation/link;
2. summarize the finding in plain language;
3. classify evidence strength;
4. state whether it:
   - supports current practice,
   - weakens current practice,
   - contradicts current practice,
   - or is interesting but not decision-relevant;
5. change the program only if the evidence or personal data justify it;
6. record the change in the relevant review document.

### Suggested change record

```text
Date:
Question:
New evidence:
Evidence strength:
Prior belief:
Updated belief:
Program change:
Review date:
```

## 22. Document philosophy

This file should become **case law for Capable 55**.

The point is not to accumulate papers. The point is to preserve the reasoning chain:

**evidence → interpretation → individual experiment → observed response → program decision.**

That makes the system portable across ChatGPT, Claude, Codex, Hermes or whatever model is best in the future — and makes it possible to update the plan without losing why earlier decisions were made.
